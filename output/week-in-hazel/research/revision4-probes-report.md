## Suggested Probes feature copy (approximately 310 words)

### Probes IV reaches dev

Andrew Blinn's Probes IV landed on September 8, merged by Cyrus Omar. The branch brings a larger inspection surface to everyday programming: a sample can expand into a multiline drawer directly below its source line. Each probe has its own drawer state, and values longer than fifteen rows scroll within that space.

The Cards example shows why more room helps. In insertion sort, `insert` compares a card's rank with the first card in a hand, then either places it before that hand or recurses through the remaining cards. Probes show the comparison and intermediate hands beside the corresponding expressions. With Alexander Bandukwala's renderer, those hands appear as cards, so readers can follow how each recursive call changes the order without decoding a list of suit-and-rank pairs.

The pictures remain part of the sample display. Small rich views fit inside the chip; selecting a taller view opens the drawer. Automatic rich views are on by default where an applicable renderer fits, and double-clicking can return a probe to text. Tables remain an explicit choice. The next page covers problems reported in the drawer interactions after this landed.

Automatic probing also gains a third mode. Off leaves placement manual; Caret follows the top-level definition under the cursor; All places probes across the program, one per source row. The sidebar exposes all three, while Cmd/Ctrl-P switches between Off and All. A two-green palette distinguishes the focal call from other samples.

Several fixes address the steps between placing a probe and reading it. Changing the probe set now refreshes analysis instead of reusing cached evaluation that lacks the new sample. Step-into reaches higher-order calls and partial applications, and left/right sample navigation scrolls its selection into view. Sample menus open by right- or alt-click rather than appearing on hover. Together these changes make it easier to choose a call, inspect its arguments and follow its result through the program.

## Suggested follow-up copy (approximately 180 words)

### Three drawer reports, one deeper regression

Three September 10 reports identify gaps in the new rich-table drawer. Closing a tall table can leave the renderer selected after its picture has disappeared: the menu still offers “Hide table,” and recovering the view requires hiding it and selecting it again (#2519). Inside the drawer, the sample menu itself is unavailable, removing access to pinning, stepping and renderer choices from that surface (#2520).

A third report isolates a placement bug to short tables. The column menu expects to anchor beneath its header, but a positioning rule still belongs to the retired modal. It consequently appears below the entire drawer. Tall scrolling tables take another CSS path and avoid that particular problem (#2521).

The performance report has a different trigger. In #2524, a recursive sum with an ascription on each call exhausts memory at 12,800 calls after Probes IV; the reporter's pre-merge build completes. Removing the ascription also restores linear behavior. The diagnosis points to repeatedly copying and retaining call-stack identifiers during evaluation. These were open reports at the issue snapshot; their timings and reproductions were not independently rerun for the zine.

## Small-story copy options

### CI: the failure that caused a needless revert (approximately 115 words)

Matthew Hammer's September 8 comment gives #2482 a concrete reason to matter. His team had reverted a good Fumola commit after an unrelated evaluator property failed. Alexander's merged change disables two unstable randomized properties, keeps their bodies compiling, and runs fixed counterexamples instead. Those checks currently expect disagreement; if the examples begin to agree, they fail with a reminder to restore the properties.

That reduces misleading failures without resolving the evaluator bugs. Matthew verified the approach locally, but also noted its limits: the checks accept any disagreement, and the separate Pattern equivalence test can still exhaust memory. The useful outcome is a more interpretable failure signal, with the remaining gaps named.
